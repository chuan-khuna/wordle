One Game shows you a story. To compare Strategies you need all of them. A <Term>Benchmark</Term> plays a <Term>Game</Term> for every word on the <Term>Answer List</Term> with one <Term>Strategy</Term>: 2,315 Games.

For each Game we write down the <Term>Guess Count</Term>, the number of Guesses needed to Win. Add them up and you get the <Term>Guess Distribution</Term>, how many Games ended at each Guess Count, plus the number of Losses. The mean over the won Games is the <Term>Average Guess Count</Term>.

```python
from collections import Counter

def benchmark(solver, opener, **strategy):
    counts = Counter()
    for answer in solver.answers:
        game = Game(answer)            # the Guess Limit is six
        candidates = solver.all_candidates()
        word = opener
        while not game.is_over:
            guess = game.guess(word)
            candidates = solver.narrow(candidates, guess.word, pack(guess.feedback))
            if not game.is_over:
                word = solver.suggest(candidates, **strategy).word
        counts[len(game.state) if game.is_won else "lost"] += 1
    return counts
```

## What it shows

**Expected Information over all words** starts with <Word>tarse</Word>. It never loses. Its Average Guess Count is **3.43**: 61 Games in 2 Guesses, 1,256 in 3, 943 in 4, 53 in 5 and 2 in 6. Three Guesses is the most common result, and almost every Game is over by the fourth.

**Expected Candidates over all words** starts with <Word>roate</Word>. It never loses either, and averages **3.48**. It is slightly slower, but it is steadier: no Game takes six Guesses.

**Expected Information over Candidates only** starts with <Word>raise</Word>. It is the Strategy from the last Article. Its average is **3.58**, and it **loses 11 Games**, listed under the chart, among them <Word>match</Word> and <Word>watch</Word>. These are the words with look-alike families, the <Word>_atch</Word> trap and others like it. The average counts the won Games only, so the real cost is a little higher than 3.58.

Every one of the 11 Losses is such a trap. After two or three Guesses the Candidates are a family that shares most of its letters, and the Solver guesses its members one at a time until it runs out of Guesses.

On the <Word>_atch</Word> and <Word>_o_er</Word> boards two Answers are lost, <Word>match</Word> and <Word>foyer</Word> on the seventh row and <Word>watch</Word> and <Word>goner</Word> on the eighth.

The big gain comes from one change: letting the Solver play a word that cannot win. It takes the Losses from 11 to 0 and the average down by 0.15 Guesses. (The Opener differs too, but the trap is what costs the Games.) Which measure the Solver uses matters less: Expected Information and Expected Candidates are within 0.05 Guesses of each other.
