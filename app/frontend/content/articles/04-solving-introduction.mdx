The first Part built the game. This Part builds a player, and it starts with one question: why is one first Guess better than another?

Before the first Guess, the Answer can be any of 2,315 words. After it, you get one of up to 243 Feedbacks. After that reply, you want as few possible Answers as you can get. The words that can still be the Answer are the <Term of="Candidate">Candidates</Term>, and all of them together are the <Term>Candidate Set</Term>. The next Article defines them exactly.

Your Guess does not choose the Feedback that you get. The Answer chooses it. But the Guess does choose how the reply divides the 2,315 words. Each possible Answer gives one Feedback, so the Guess puts every word into a group, one group for each Feedback. These groups are the <Term>Feedback Split</Term>.

Compare two Guesses. <Word>crane</Word> puts the 2,315 words into 142 groups. Its largest group holds the 263 words that contain no <Letter>c</Letter>, <Letter>r</Letter>, <Letter>a</Letter>, <Letter>n</Letter> or <Letter>e</Letter>. <Word>fuzzy</Word> makes only 34 groups, and its largest group, the words with none of its letters, holds 1,352 words. On average, the reply to <Word>crane</Word> leaves far fewer words than the reply to <Word>fuzzy</Word>.

So a good Guess makes many groups, and the groups are small and of similar size. The next step is to put a number on that.

## Two ways to count

Let $N$ be the number of Candidates. A Guess divides them into groups of sizes $n_1, n_2, \ldots$, one group for each Feedback that the Guess can get. If each Candidate has the same chance to be the Answer, the chance of the group for Feedback $i$ is its share of the words:

$$
p_i = \frac{n_i}{N}
$$

The main idea is this. **We judge a Guess by what is left after one Feedback, and we take the average over the split.** Each group gets a value, and the average gives each value the weight $p_i$. Two values for a group are natural.

The first value is the size of the group, $n_i$, which is the number of Candidates left. The average of this value is the <Term>Expected Candidates</Term>, the number of possible Answers that we expect to be left. Lower is better.

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{left}] = \sum_i p_i \, n_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i n_i^2
$$

The second value is how much the group cuts the Candidate Set. A group of $n_i$ words out of $N$ cuts the set by a factor of $N / n_i$. We count that factor in halvings, and the name of one halving is a bit. A group with half of the words is worth one bit. A group with a quarter of the words is worth two bits. The average of this value is the <Term>Expected Information</Term>. Higher is better.

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{bits}] = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{N}{n_i} = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i
$$

The two formulas have the same shape. Each one is an average over the same Feedback Split. The only difference is the value of each group: its size, or the number of halvings.

Try a small example. 10 Candidates divide into groups of 5, 3, 1 and 1, so the chances are 0.5, 0.3, 0.1 and 0.1.

```python
from math import log2

def expected_left(sizes):
    n = sum(sizes)
    return sum(c * c for c in sizes) / n

def expected_bits(sizes):
    n = sum(sizes)
    return sum(c / n * log2(n / c) for c in sizes)

expected_left([5, 3, 1, 1])   # 3.6
expected_bits([5, 3, 1, 1])   # 1.685
```

On average, we expect 3.6 Candidates to be left, and the Guess is worth 1.685 bits. <MarginNote>The Solver uses the same two formulas, for all words at the same time.</MarginNote>

## They can disagree

Both formulas measure the same split, so why use two? Because they give a large group a different weight. In the first formula, a large group counts with its square, so it has a large effect. In the second formula, a large group counts only through a logarithm, so its effect is smaller.

The two formulas do select different words. Over the full Answer List, the Guess with the highest Expected Information is <Word>tarse</Word>: 5.947 bits, with 66.0 Candidates left on average. The Guess with the lowest Expected Candidates is <Word>roate</Word>: 60.4 Candidates left, but only 5.883 bits. <Word>tarse</Word> makes more groups, 147 against 126, but its largest group is also larger, 227 words against 195.

Neither measure is wrong. Each one is an answer to the question "what is a good split?". The rest of this Series lets you try both. The next Article shows how to find the Candidates. The Article after it makes the Feedback Split of any Guess and shows both numbers.
