Before the first Guess, the Answer could be any of 2,315 words. After it, you get one of up to 243 Feedbacks. What makes one first Guess better than another?

Think of what you want to be holding after the first reply: as few possible Answers as you can get. The words that could still be the Answer are the <Term of="Candidate">Candidates</Term>, and together they are the <Term>Candidate Set</Term>. The next Article makes that exact.

A Guess does not choose which Feedback you get. The Answer does. What the Guess chooses is how the 2,315 words are sorted by that reply. Every possible Answer gives some Feedback, so the Guess sorts all of them into groups, one group for each Feedback. That sorting is the <Term>Feedback Split</Term>.

Compare two Guesses. <Word>crane</Word> sorts the 2,315 words into 142 groups. The biggest group, with no <Letter>c</Letter>, <Letter>r</Letter>, <Letter>a</Letter>, <Letter>n</Letter> or <Letter>e</Letter> at all, holds 263 words. <Word>fuzzy</Word> sorts them into only 34 groups, and its biggest group, with none of its letters, holds 1,352 words. The reply to <Word>crane</Word> on average leaves you with far fewer words than the reply to <Word>fuzzy</Word>.

So a good Guess makes many groups and keeps them small and even. Now let's count that.

## Two ways to count

Call the number of Candidates $N$. A Guess splits them into groups of sizes $n_1, n_2, \ldots$, one group for each Feedback it could get. The group for Feedback $i$ is reached with probability

$$
p_i = \frac{n_i}{N}
$$

because if every Candidate is equally likely to be the Answer, then the chance of landing in a group is its share of the words.

Here is the key idea. **We judge a Guess by what is left after one Feedback, and we average over the split.** Each group has a score, and the average is weighted by $p_i$. There are two natural scores for a group.

The first score is its size, $n_i$: how many Candidates are left. Averaging it gives the <Term>Expected Candidates</Term>, the number of possible Answers we expect to be left. Lower is better.

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{left}] = \sum_i p_i \, n_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i n_i^2
$$

The second score is how many times it cuts the Candidate Set down. A group of size $n_i$ out of $N$ cuts the set by a factor of $N / n_i$, and we count that in halvings, which are called bits. A group that is half the words is worth one bit. A group that is a quarter is worth two. Averaging that gives the <Term>Expected Information</Term>. Higher is better.

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{bits}] = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{N}{n_i} = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i
$$

The two formulas share a shape. Both are an expectation over the same Feedback Split. They differ only in the score of each group: its size, or how many halvings it saves.

A small example makes it concrete. Say 10 Candidates split into groups of 5, 3, 1 and 1. The chances are 0.5, 0.3, 0.1 and 0.1.

```python
from math import log2

def expected_left(sizes):
    n = sum(sizes)
    return sum(c * c for c in sizes) / n

def expected_bits(sizes):
    n = sum(sizes)
    return sum(c / n * log2(n / c) for c in sizes)

expected_left([5, 3, 1, 1])   # 3.6
expected_bits([5, 3, 1, 1])   # 1.685
```

On average we expect 3.6 Candidates left, and the Guess is worth 1.685 bits. <MarginNote>The Solver uses the same two formulas, just for every word at once.</MarginNote>

## They can disagree

If both measure the same split, why have two? Because they treat a big group differently. A big group counts with its square in the first formula, so it hurts a lot. In the second it counts only through a logarithm, so it hurts less.

The two really do pick different words. Over the whole Answer List, the Guess with the most Expected Information is <Word>tarse</Word>: 5.947 bits, leaving 66.0 Candidates on average. The Guess with the fewest Expected Candidates is <Word>roate</Word>: 60.4 left, but only 5.883 bits. <Word>tarse</Word> makes more groups, 147 against 126, but one of them is bigger, 227 words against 195.

Neither is wrong. They are two answers to the question "what does a good split look like?". The rest of this Series builds the tools to try both. The next Article shows how to find the Candidates, and the one after that builds the Feedback Split for any Guess and shows both numbers.
